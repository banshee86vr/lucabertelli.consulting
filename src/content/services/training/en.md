## Why catalogue training often is not enough

A standard Kubernetes course teaches Kubernetes. That is useful, and it is also why certifications exist. But the following week participants go back to a cluster configured in its own particular way, with corporate constraints, inherited decisions and a pipeline that looks nothing like the one in the exercise. The gap between the example and reality is where most of the learning evaporates.

There is also a question of level. In nearly every group I train the internal spread is wide: two or three people already have experience, the rest are starting from zero. A single programme bores the former and loses the latter.

## How I build a programme

Before defining the curriculum I talk to the technical leads and check the participants' actual level, usually with a short questionnaire. It tells me how much time to spend on fundamentals and which topics can be assumed. It costs very little and noticeably changes how much the days deliver.

The programme alternates explanation and practice in short blocks. The lab part uses an environment aligned with yours: if you work with GitLab CI the lab is on GitLab CI, if your cluster runs on AKS the exercises run on AKS. That removes the mental translation step that usually prevents people from applying what they learned.

Sessions are spread over time where possible, rather than compressed into one intensive week. Between sessions participants try what they saw on real work, and the next session starts from the difficulties that surfaced. That is when the programme produces the most value.

## What remains afterwards

Course material and recordings stay with the company and can be reused for future hires. A few weeks later I schedule a follow-up session dedicated to the problems the team ran into in the meantime: it is short, but it is the part that really consolidates adoption.

The experience behind these programmes is operational before it is didactic. I worked on the same problems as a DevOps Engineer, as a Cloud Native Engineering Manager and as a consultant, and the examples I use in the room come from there.

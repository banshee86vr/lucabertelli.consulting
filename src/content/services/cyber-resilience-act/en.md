## The problem I usually find

Most teams I meet on this topic have already read the regulation, or a summary of it, and have concluded that the hard part is the paperwork. It is not. The hard part is that the obligations describe a process that runs for the entire support period of the product, and the current delivery process was never designed to produce that kind of evidence continuously.

The typical starting point: an SBOM was generated once, by hand, for a customer questionnaire, and nobody knows which build it describes. The vulnerability scanner in the pipeline reports two hundred findings per release, most of them in code the product never executes, so the team has stopped reading it. Releases are not signed, or they are signed with a key that lives in a CI variable. And if a customer asked tomorrow whether a given CVE affects version 3.2, someone would spend two days finding out.

## How I work

I start from what the pipeline already produces, not from the text of the regulation. Annex I, Part II lists what a manufacturer must be able to do with vulnerabilities: identify and document them, fix them without undue delay, disclose them, distribute updates securely. Each of those points maps to a build step or a process that either exists or does not, and the gap assessment is exactly that mapping.

The work is then sequenced by return. SBOM generation first, because it is cheap and everything else depends on it: without a reliable inventory of what is in each release, monitoring and disclosure have nothing to stand on. Signing and provenance next, so the inventory can be tied to a specific artifact. Monitoring and VEX third, because that is where the ongoing cost lives and where the team needs a workflow rather than another tool.

The tooling comes from what you already run. GitLab CI and GitHub Actions both have adequate options for generating SBOMs and signing artifacts; the differences between them matter far less than whether the output is reviewed and kept.

## Signing, SBOM, VEX and VDR in practice

**Signing and provenance.** Every artifact that leaves the pipeline, whether a container image, a package or a firmware bundle, is signed, and a provenance attestation records which commit, which pipeline run and which inputs produced it. Signing keys are not stored in the pipeline: they are issued at runtime or held in a secrets manager and used through short-lived credentials. This is the same work I do on the [SecDevOps side](/en/services/secdevops-cicd-consulting/), applied with the CRA's secure-distribution requirement in mind.

**SBOM.** Generated at build time, per artifact, in CycloneDX or SPDX, and stored next to the artifact it describes, with the signature covering both. The regulation asks for at least the top-level dependencies; in practice a full transitive inventory costs nothing extra and is what makes the monitoring useful.

**VEX and VDR.** A scanner matching a full SBOM against vulnerability databases produces a long list, and most of it is noise: vulnerable functions the product never calls, components present but unused, issues that only apply on another platform. A VEX statement records the judgement (affected, not affected, fixed, under investigation) together with its justification, so the same question is not investigated again every week. The VDR is assembled from those statements: which known vulnerabilities concern this release, what their status is, what the customer should do. Both are generated from data rather than written in a text editor, and both are versioned with the release.

**Monitoring.** The SBOMs of every release still inside its support period are checked continuously against new vulnerability data. When a match appears, the alert goes to the team that owns the component, with the VEX history attached. This is what makes the reporting deadlines realistic: when an actively exploited vulnerability is published, the question "are we affected, and where" already has an answer.

## What this means for the reporting obligations

From 11 September 2026 manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents through the single reporting platform run by ENISA: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, a notification within 72 hours, and a final report once a fix is available (within 14 days for vulnerabilities, one month for incidents). None of these windows can be met by starting an investigation when the clock starts. They can be met when the inventory, the exposure assessment and the update channel already exist and are exercised on every release. That is what the work above builds.

## What I do not do

I do not classify your product, choose the conformity assessment route or write the declaration of conformity. Those are decisions for you, your legal counsel and, when the class requires it, a notified body. What I make sure of is that when those decisions are taken, the technical documentation they depend on is generated by your process and describes what you actually ship.
